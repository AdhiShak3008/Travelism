"Hi everyone, my name is Aditya Shakya. Today, I'm going to walk you through Travelism, 
an autonomous travel-planning system that I designed and built.

Let's start with the problem. Planning a trip today is painful. If you want to plan a real 
vacation—say, a week exploring Kyoto or an off-grid trip to Tawang in the Himalayas—you 
usually end up with twenty browser tabs open. You have TripAdvisor for spots, Google Maps 
for routes, booking sites for hotels, airline websites for flights, and outdated blog posts 
for travel tips. It takes hours of manual work just to piece together a basic itinerary.

Now, you might think: why not just ask ChatGPT? 
Well, general-purpose chatbots fail at travel. If you ask a generic AI to plan your trip, 
it hallucinates. It suggests attractions that closed two years ago. It ignores geography—like 
putting three places in one day that are a six-hour drive apart. And worst of all, it has 
no concept of real-world math: it will promise you a luxury vacation for five hundred dollars, 
or add up hotel nights and flight tickets completely wrong.

This problem is critical because travel is high-stakes. People spend real savings and take 
rare vacation days. If your itinerary sends you to a closed mountain pass, or book you into 
a hotel with terrible cleanliness, your whole holiday is ruined. 

I built Travelism to solve this: by combining real-time web-scouting agents with deterministic 
math, geofencing, and an interactive AI Concierge."

[SPOKEN SCRIPT]:
"Here is how Travelism is structured under the hood.

Instead of one giant AI prompt doing everything, Travelism uses a swarm of specialized 
agents. Each agent has one clear job:
- 'Scout' finds real attractions and local food recommendations.
- 'Middleman' checks that spots actually exist and are inside the right city.
- 'Lens' grabs verified, high-quality photos.
- 'Pillow' inspects hotel cleanliness and bathroom scores.
- 'Weather Witch' checks seasonal weather and packing needs.
- 'Penny Pincher' calculates budgets and currency conversions.
- And 'Concierge' is our conversational assistant that chats with the traveler and edits the trip.

When designing this, I considered two other architectures and rejected both:

First, I considered a 'Single Mega-Prompt'—one prompt with ten function calls trying to do 
research, hotels, weather, and budget all at once. I rejected this because it was slow, 
expensive, and inaccurate. Large prompts get easily confused and hallucinate when you give 
them too many competing tasks.

Second, I tried an unconstrained multi-agent chat—where agents chat freely with each other. 
I rejected this because open loops are unpredictable. Agents talk back and forth forever, 
latency explodes, and costs run out of control.

So I chose a Hybrid Pipeline: a linear, directed pipeline to gather the data fast, 
Server-Sent Events to stream progress to the user in real time, and LangGraph for 
self-correcting loops when an itinerary breaks constraints.

For models: we use Groq-hosted open-source models—specifically `openai/gpt-oss-120b` for 
deep reasoning, and `openai/gpt-oss-20b` as a fast fallback. Groq runs at over 250 tokens 
per second. When planning a vacation, speed is everything. A sub-second response keeps the 
user engaged."

PART 3: WHERE WE USE CODE VS. WHERE WE USE LLMS (04:30 – 07:00)
Target: ~320 words | ~2.5 minutes
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[ON SCREEN]: 
Open `src/lib/engine.ts` in VS Code. 
Scroll to and highlight `computeCosts` (around line 50) and `buildItinerary`.

[SPOKEN SCRIPT]:
"A central rule of building reliable AI products is knowing when to use an LLM, and when 
NOT to use an LLM.

In Travelism, I drew a very strict boundary between deterministic code and AI reasoning.

Deterministic TypeScript code handles three things:
Number one: All financial math and budgeting. 
Never let an LLM do multiplication or addition. Look at this function, `computeCosts`. 
When a traveler changes group size from two to four people, or switches currency from USD 
to INR, that calculation is 100% deterministic code. Hotel rates multiplied by nights, 
flights multiplied by passengers, plus taxes. It is mathematically impossible for Travelism 
to hallucinate your budget.

Number two: Calendar timelines and day slotting. 
Figuring out Day 1, Day 2, and Day 3, spacing out driving times, and making sure morning 
activities come before dinner—that is all handled by deterministic scheduling algorithms 
in `buildItinerary`.

Number three: Geofencing. 
If an AI suggests a restaurant, our code checks its coordinates against a geographical 
boundary to confirm it is actually inside the destination.

So where DO we use LLMs?
We use LLMs where human flexibility is needed:
1. Understanding ambiguous requests—like 'a peaceful mountain getaway with authentic food'.
2. Reading raw website text to pull out interesting stories and local tips.
3. And running our AI Concierge—translating a user's natural request like 'Make day 3 a beach 
day' into a clean, structured JSON action that our code can execute safely.

This split gives us the best of both worlds: human-like flexibility on the front, with 
rock-solid mathematical reliability underneath."

[SPOKEN SCRIPT]:
"Now let's look at the prompts themselves. 
On the left is Concierge Prompt V1 from our early build. 
On the right is our Current Production Prompt.

We didn't rewrite this prompt from scratch every week. Instead, we observed real failure 
modes in production, and tuned the prompt with focused, high-leverage rules.

Let's look at four specific problems we discovered and how we fixed them:

First: The Lazy One-Liner Problem.
In V1, when a user asked: 'Audit my itinerary and tell me what to improve', the model would 
execute the change in the background, but reply with a dry one-liner: 'Done! I have updated 
your itinerary.' That is a terrible user experience.
So in the current prompt, we added Rule 1:
'CRITICAL REQUIREMENT: NEVER return a dry or lazy 1-liner like \"Done!\". ALWAYS provide a 
comprehensive, beautifully structured markdown reply with bold headers, timings, and 
explanations for what you evaluated or changed.'
Now, the AI walks you through its exact reasoning.

Second: Hallucinating Openings during Closures.
In V1, if a road was snowed out or a temple closed on Mondays, the Concierge would still 
schedule it. 
To fix this, we connected our Middleman agent directly into the prompt. Now, the prompt 
receives an alert called `MIDDLEMAN ARBITER FACT-CHECK ENFORCEMENT`. If a closure exists, 
the prompt is instructed: 'You MUST incorporate this closure into your answer.'

Third: Conversation Amnesia.
In V1, if you told the Concierge in message 1 that you are vegan, by message 4 it suggested 
a steakhouse. 
In the current prompt, we injected an active memory block: `ACTIVE TRAVELER MEMORY`, and we 
added a `memoryDelta` field in the return JSON. Now, learned preferences stay with you 
across the entire trip.

Fourth: Currency Confusion.
When a user switched their display currency to Euros or Dollars, V1 kept quoting Indian Rupees. 
We fixed this with one clear instruction: 'Active Display Currency: ${currency}. STRICT 
REQUIREMENT: All prices quoted in your conversation MUST use this currency.'

This is how we tuned the system: finding exact user pain points, and fixing them with 
targeted constraints."

Here we have an active trip to Tawang. I'm opening the Concierge and asking:
'Make day 3 a relaxed culinary and cafe day, and check if I need any permits.'

Watch what happens:
First, notice the response. It doesn't give a lazy one-liner. It explains why resting is 
important at high altitude, breaks down the local Monpa food you should try, and reminds 
us that Tawang requires an Inner Line Permit.
Second, look at the schedule: Day 3 was automatically updated with morning cafes, afternoon 
rest, and traditional dinner stops. The UI and the trip state mutated in real time.

Now, what real challenges did we hit building this?

The first challenge was Latency and Cold Starts. 
Running live searches and LLM reasoning took 12 seconds per trip. Users hated waiting. 
We fixed this by building a two-tier caching system: an in-memory L1 cache, plus an Upstash 
Redis L2 cache. Common searches now load in under 300 milliseconds.

The second challenge was Cross-Device Session Loss. 
Earlier, if a user started planning on desktop, then opened an incognito tab or checked on 
mobile, their trip reset to zero because it only lived in browser local storage. 
We solved this by building a cloud persistence endpoint backed by Neon Postgres. Now, as 
soon as you log in on any device, your in-progress trip, spots, and notes restore instantly."

silently break it?

In our submission package, under `02_evaluation/`, we have four automated regression tests. 
Let me run one right now: `node submission-package/02_evaluation/test-langgraph.mjs`.

Look at the terminal:
This test runs our LangGraph state machine. It simulates building an itinerary. 
On iteration 1, the Auditor node spots an issue: transit duration between spots exceeds 
5 hours. Instead of returning that broken plan to the user, our routing logic catches the 
warning, loops back to the Synthesizer node, and rebuilds the day. Iteration 2 passes all 
feasibility checks, and the plan is finalized and saved to checkpoint storage. This test 
guarantees our self-correction loop never crashes or gets stuck in infinite loops.

Our other tests catch real historical bugs:
- `test-investigate.mjs` checks our live streaming pipeline and stops flight hallucinations. 
  In an early test, a flight from Hyderabad to Miami was hallucinated as a two-hour non-stop! 
  This test verifies that real layovers and flight durations are used.
- `test-profile.mjs` verifies that traveler profiles work. It sends the same destination—Jaipur—to 
  a budget vegetarian traveler and a luxury traveler, and asserts that hotel prices and food 
  recommendations actually change.
- And `test-images.mjs` verifies that place photos are real, verified images from travel blogs 
  and CDNs, not broken links or generic icons."
